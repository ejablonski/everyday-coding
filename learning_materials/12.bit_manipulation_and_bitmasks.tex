% ============================================================================
\section{Foundations and Core Concepts}
% ============================================================================

At the physical level, computer hardware stores numbers as rows of microscopic transistor switches. Each switch---called a \textbf{bit} (\emph{binary digit})---holds either $0$ (OFF) or $1$ (ON).

Imagine a row of $8$ light switches on a wall. Each switch flips independently, so $8$ switches encode $2^8 = 256$ distinct patterns; a 32-bit integer is simply $32$ switches encoding $2^{32} = 4{,}294{,}967{,}296$ distinct states.

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Foundations]
$b_k$ & The bit value ($0$ or $1$) at 0-based index $k$ & Rightmost bit is $b_0$ \\
LSB / MSB & Least Significant Bit ($k=0$, weight $1$) / Most Significant Bit & Determines parity / magnitude \& sign \\
$2^k$ & Positional weight of bit index $k$ & Constructed safely via \texttt{1ULL << k} \\
$\sim x$ & Bitwise NOT: flips every $0 \to 1$ and $1 \to 0$ & One's complement of $x$ \\
\end{notationbox}

\subsection{Positional Binary Representation and Powers of Two}

In everyday decimal (base-$10$), digits carry weights $10^0, 10^1, 10^2$ from right to left. In binary (base-$2$), each bit $b_k \in \{0, 1\}$ carries weight $2^k$.

\begin{formulabox}[Unsigned Binary Value]
$$V = \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} b_k \cdot 2^k = b_0 \cdot 2^0 + b_1 \cdot 2^1 + \dots + b_{N-1} \cdot 2^{N-1}$$
\textbf{Read this as:} Walk from right (bit $0$) to left (bit $N-1$). Whenever a switch is ON ($b_k = 1$), add its power of two ($2^k$) to the total.
\end{formulabox}

\examplebeat For $101011_2$: switches $0, 1, 3, 5$ are ON, so $2^5 + 2^3 + 2^1 + 2^0 = 32 + 8 + 2 + 1 = 43_{10}$.

\subsection{Anatomy of an Integer and Two's Complement Mechanics}

Bit $0$ (the \textbf{LSB}) has weight $1$; because every higher bit is a multiple of $2$, the LSB alone decides whether an integer is odd ($1$) or even ($0$). The highest bit position (the \textbf{MSB}) dictates magnitude---and in signed integers, it encodes the sign.

\painbeat How should a CPU store negative numbers? Using a separate sign bit (``sign-magnitude'') creates two distinct zeros ($+0$ and $-0$) and forces hardware engineers to build separate circuits for addition and subtraction.

\ahabeat Modern CPUs use \textbf{two's complement}: give the single highest bit $b_{N-1}$ a \emph{negative} weight ($-2^{N-1}$) while every other bit keeps its normal positive weight. One single unsigned adder circuit now handles both signed and unsigned addition automatically, and zero is unique!

\begin{formulabox}[Two's Complement Value and Negation]
$$V_{\text{signed}} = -b_{N-1} \cdot 2^{N-1} + \sum_{k=0}^{N-2} b_k \cdot 2^k, \qquad -x = (\sim x) + 1$$
\textbf{Read this as:} To negate any number in binary, flip every switch ($\sim x$) and add $1$. Notice that flipping every bit gives $x + (\sim x) = 11\dots1_2 = -1$, so adding $1$ yields $x + (\sim x) + 1 = 0$, proving $(\sim x) + 1 = -x$.
\end{formulabox}

\examplebeat Negate $x = 6$ in 4-bit two's complement ($0110_2$): invert all bits $\sim(0110_2) = 1001_2$, add $1$ to get $1010_2$. Check value: $-(1 \cdot 8) + 0 + (1 \cdot 2) + 0 = -6$.

\begin{watchoutbox}[Watch Out --- Fixed-Width Limits and the 64-Bit Shift Trap]
Always prefer \texttt{<cstdint>} fixed-width types: \texttt{uint32\_t} ($[0, 2^{32}{-}1]$), \texttt{int32\_t} ($[-2^{31}, 2^{31}{-}1]$), \texttt{uint64\_t} ($[0, 2^{64}{-}1]$), and \texttt{int64\_t} ($[-2^{63}, 2^{63}{-}1]$). Plain integer literals like \texttt{1} are 32-bit signed \texttt{int}, so writing \texttt{1 << 31} or \texttt{1 << 40} is \textbf{Undefined Behavior}! Always write \texttt{1ULL << k} (and ensure $0 \le k < 64$) for 64-bit masks.
\end{watchoutbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Trace}{Two's Complement and LSB Isolation}{ex1}
Convert $-13$ into an 8-bit two's complement binary string (start from $+13 = 00001101_2$, invert, add $1$). Then compute $x \mathbin{\&} (-x)$ on $x = 12$ ($00001100_2$) in 8-bit binary and verify that it isolates $2^2 = 4$.
\end{tryitbox}

\section{Bitwise Operators, Idioms, and C++23 \texttt{<bit>}}
% ============================================================================

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used for Bitwise Operations]
$\mathbin{\&},\; \mid,\; \oplus,\; \sim$ & Bitwise AND, OR, XOR, and NOT & Act column-by-column with zero carry \\
$\ll,\; \gg$ & Left shift ($\times 2^k$) and Right shift ($\lfloor x / 2^k \rfloor$ unsigned) & Keep shift $0 \le k < \text{bit width}$ \\

\end{notationbox}

\subsection{The Six Operators and Four Elemental Primitives}

Bitwise operators act simultaneously on every bit column independently: \textbf{AND} (\texttt{\&}) masks/filters ($1$ only when both bits are $1$), \textbf{OR} (\texttt{|}) merges/sets ($1$ if either bit is $1$), \textbf{XOR} (\texttt{\textasciicircum}) adds mod $2$ without carry ($1$ if bits differ), \textbf{NOT} (\texttt{\textasciitilde}) flips all bits, and \textbf{shifts} (\texttt{<<}, \texttt{>>}) slide bits left or right.

\subsection{Lowest Set Bit Isolation: \texorpdfstring{$x \mathbin{\&} (-x)$}{x \& (-x)}}

\painbeat Data structures like Fenwick Trees (Binary Indexed Trees) and XOR-partitioning algorithms need the \emph{exact lowest $1$-bit} of $x$ (zeroing all other bits) without scanning bit-by-bit in a loop.

\ahabeat Look at what two's complement negation $-x = (\sim x) + 1$ does to the binary pattern $x = [\text{prefix}]\,1\,[0\dots 0]$: flipping all bits turns the trailing zeros into trailing ones and turns the lowest $1$-bit into $0$. Adding $1$ sends a carry ripple straight across all trailing ones until it stops at that exact bit position---restoring it to $1$ while leaving the entire higher prefix inverted!

\examplebeat Take $x = 44 = 00101100_2$. Flipping gives $\sim x = 11010011_2$. Adding $1$ ripples across bits $0$ and $1$ and lands on bit $2$: $-x = 11010100_2$. Now ANDing $x \mathbin{\&} (-x)$ cancels every bit above bit $2$ (because prefix AND $\sim$prefix $= 0$) and isolates $00000100_2 = 4$!

\begin{formulabox}[Lowest Set Bit Isolation]
$$\text{lowest\_set\_bit}(x) = x \mathbin{\&} (-x) = x \mathbin{\&} (\sim x + 1)$$
\textbf{Read this as:} For any non-zero unsigned integer $x$, keep only its rightmost $1$-bit ($2^k$) and clear every other bit in a single CPU cycle.
\end{formulabox}

\subsection{Brian Kernighan's Algorithm: \texorpdfstring{$x \mathbin{\&} (x - 1)$}{x \& (x - 1)}}

\painbeat Counting set bits by looping over all $64$ bit positions wastes time when only $2$ bits are ON.

\ahabeat Subtracting $1$ from $x$ borrows across every trailing zero until it hits the lowest $1$-bit: that lowest $1$-bit flips to $0$, all trailing zeros flip to $1$, and every higher bit stays identical. ANDing $x$ with $x - 1$ therefore \textbf{erases strictly the lowest $1$-bit} in one step!

\begin{formulabox}[Clear Lowest Set Bit and Power-of-Two Test]
$$\text{clear\_lowest}(x) = x \mathbin{\&} (x - 1), \qquad \text{is\_power\_of\_two}(x) \iff (x > 0) \land \big((x \mathbin{\&} (x - 1)) == 0\big)$$
\textbf{Read this as:} Repeatedly applying $x \leftarrow x \mathbin{\&} (x - 1)$ counts set bits in $O(K)$ iterations where $K$ is the number of $1$-bits. A positive number is a power of two if and only if erasing its single $1$-bit leaves $0$.
\end{formulabox}

\subsection{XOR Algebraic Invariants and Prefix XOR Queries}

\painbeat Finding a unique element among duplicate pairs with a hash map takes $O(N)$ extra memory, and computing the XOR of $Q$ subarrays $[L, R]$ naively takes $O(Q \cdot N)$ time.

\ahabeat XOR is simply \textbf{addition modulo $2$ without carry} on each bit column. Because $0 + 0 \equiv 0$, $1 + 1 \equiv 0 \pmod 2$, every element is its own inverse:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Commutative \& Associative}: order and grouping never matter ($A \oplus B = B \oplus A$).
    \item \textbf{Identity}: $A \oplus 0 = A$.
    \item \textbf{Self-Inverse (Involution)}: $A \oplus A = 0$, so $(A \oplus B) \oplus B = A$.
\end{enumerate}

Just like a prefix sum array cancels a prefix by subtraction, a \textbf{prefix XOR array} $P[0] = 0,\; P[i] = a_0 \oplus \dots \oplus a_{i-1}$ cancels any unwanted prefix by XORing with itself!

\begin{formulabox}[O(1) Range XOR Query]
$$\text{range\_xor}(L, R) = a_L \oplus a_{L+1} \oplus \dots \oplus a_R = P[L] \oplus P[R + 1]$$
\textbf{Read this as:} XORing the prefix up to $R$ with the prefix before $L$ forces every element before $L$ to appear \emph{twice} and annihilate to $0$, leaving strictly the subarray $[L, R]$.
\end{formulabox}

\subsection{Independent Bit Column Accounting and Conservation}

A foundational superpower of bitwise operators (\texttt{\&}, \texttt{|}, \texttt{\textasciicircum}) is that \textbf{bit columns never carry into neighboring columns}. Bit $k$ of $A \mathbin{\&} B$, $A \mid B$, or $A \oplus B$ depends \emph{solely} on bit $k$ of $A$ and bit $k$ of $B$.

This unlocks the \textbf{Column Ledger Pattern}: count how many numbers have bit $k$ set ($\text{count}[k]$ for $k = 0 \dots 31$), solve each bit column independently, and recombine via $\sum 2^k$. Moreover, replacing $(A, B)$ with $(A \mathbin{\&} B,\; A \mid B)$ \textbf{conserves} the $1$-bit count in every column:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item $(0, 0) \to (0, 0)$ (zero $1$-bits), \quad $(1, 1) \to (1, 1)$ (two $1$-bits), \quad $(1, 0) \to (0, 1)$ (one $1$-bit migrates from \texttt{AND} to \texttt{OR}).
\end{itemize}
Because $1$-bits simply migrate without changing the total sum $A + B$, you can greedily pack all available bits into as few numbers as possible to maximize convex objectives like $\sum x_i^2$.

\subsection{Bitmask Subset Representation}

For a small universe of $N \le 20$ elements, an integer $\text{mask} \in [0, 2^N - 1]$ replaces an entire boolean vector (\texttt{std::vector<bool>}) with a single CPU register:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Empty Set ($\emptyset$)}: $\text{mask} = 0$ \qquad $|$ \qquad \textbf{Full Universe ($U$)}: $\text{mask} = (1\text{ULL} \ll N) - 1$.
    \item \textbf{Union ($S_1 \cup S_2$)}: $\text{mask}_1 \mid \text{mask}_2$ \qquad $|$ \qquad \textbf{Intersection ($S_1 \cap S_2$)}: $\text{mask}_1 \mathbin{\&} \text{mask}_2$.
    \item \textbf{Set Difference ($S_1 \setminus S_2$)}: $\text{mask}_1 \mathbin{\&} (\sim \text{mask}_2)$ \qquad $|$ \qquad \textbf{Symmetric Diff ($S_1 \triangle S_2$)}: $\text{mask}_1 \oplus \text{mask}_2$.
    \item \textbf{Cardinality ($|S|$)}: \texttt{std::popcount(mask)}.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Submask Enumeration in \texorpdfstring{$O(3^N)$}{O(3\textasciicircum N)} Time}

\painbeat Suppose a DP transition needs to iterate over every subset $\text{sub} \subseteq \text{mask}$ for every $\text{mask} \in [0, 2^N - 1]$. Testing all $2^N$ candidates for each of the $2^N$ masks takes $O(4^N)$ operations---at $N = 15$, $4^{15} \approx 1.07 \times 10^9$ (way too slow!).

\ahabeat To jump directly from one valid submask to the next smaller valid submask, subtract $1$ and immediately mask off any bit not present in $\text{mask}$: \texttt{sub = (sub - 1) \& mask}. Subtracting $1$ turns the lowest $1$-bit of \texttt{sub} into $0$ and fills all lower bits with $1$; ANDing with \texttt{mask} keeps strictly the lower bits that belong to \texttt{mask}, skipping every invalid integer in $O(1)$!

\begin{formulabox}[The $O(3^N)$ All-Submasks Bound]
$$\sum_{k=0}^{N} \binom{N}{k} 2^k = (1 + 2)^N = 3^N$$
\textbf{Read this as:} Each of the $N$ bit positions independently chooses one of \textbf{3} roles: (1) absent from both, (2) in \texttt{mask} only, or (3) in both \texttt{mask} and \texttt{sub}. At $N = 15$, $3^{15} \approx 1.43 \times 10^7$ finishes in milliseconds!
\end{formulabox}

\subsection{Fixed-Size Combinations (Gosper's Hack) and Bitmask DP}

\textbf{Gosper's Hack ($K$-Combinations in $O(1)$ per Mask):} When you need every $N$-bit mask with \emph{exactly} $K$ set bits (in lexicographical order without recursion), start at $\text{set} = (1\text{ULL} \ll K) - 1$. At each step, isolate the lowest $1$-bit $c = \text{set} \mathbin{\&} (-\text{set})$, carry across the lowest contiguous block of $1$s via $r = \text{set} + c$, and shift the displaced bits back to the bottom via \texttt{((r \textasciicircum{} set) >> (std::countr\_zero(set) + 2)) | r}. Replacing division \texttt{/ c} with a right shift by \texttt{std::countr\_zero(set)} avoids slow integer division!

\textbf{Bitmask Dynamic Programming (State Compression):} Trying all $N!$ city orderings in the Travelling Salesperson Problem (TSP) explodes at $N = 20$ ($20! \approx 2.4 \times 10^{18}$). Because future travel depends only on \emph{which subset of cities was visited} (\texttt{mask}) and \emph{where you currently stand} ($u$), compressing the visited set into a bitmask cuts complexity from $O(N!)$ to $O(N^2 \cdot 2^N)$:
$$dp[\text{mask} \mid (1\text{ULL} \ll v)][v] = \min\big(dp[\text{mask} \mid (1\text{ULL} \ll v)][v],\; dp[\text{mask}][u] + \text{cost}[u][v]\big).$$

\begin{example}[Single Unique Element via XOR Accumulation]
Find the single element appearing once in $A = [4, 1, 2, 1, 2]$ (all others appear twice) in $O(N)$ time and $O(1)$ space.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item Initialize $\text{acc} = 0$.
    \item Accumulate: $0 \oplus 4 \oplus 1 \oplus 2 \oplus 1 \oplus 2$.
    \item Regroup by commutativity and associativity: $4 \oplus (1 \oplus 1) \oplus (2 \oplus 2) = 4 \oplus 0 \oplus 0 = \mathbf{4}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Two Unique Elements via Bit Partitioning]
In $A = [1, 2, 1, 3, 2, 5]$, every element appears twice except two unique values. Find both in $O(N)$ time and $O(1)$ space.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item XOR all elements: $X = (1 \oplus 1) \oplus (2 \oplus 2) \oplus (3 \oplus 5) = 3 \oplus 5 = 0011_2 \oplus 0101_2 = 0110_2 = 6$.
    \item Isolate the lowest differing bit: $\text{diff} = X \mathbin{\&} (-X) = 6 \mathbin{\&} (-6) = 0010_2 = 2$ (bit $1$).
    \item Partition $A$ by bit $1$:
    \begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=1pt]
        \item \textbf{Group 0} (\texttt{(x \& 2) == 0}): $[1, 1, 5] \implies 1 \oplus 1 \oplus 5 = \mathbf{5}$.
        \item \textbf{Group 1} (\texttt{(x \& 2) != 0}): $[2, 3, 2] \implies 2 \oplus 3 \oplus 2 = \mathbf{3}$.
    \end{itemize}
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Result:} The two unique elements are $\mathbf{5}$ and $\mathbf{3}$.
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Population Count via Brian Kernighan's Algorithm]
Count the set bits in $x = 52 = 00110100_2$ using $x \leftarrow x \mathbin{\&} (x - 1)$.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Initialization}: $\text{count} = 0$, $x = 52 = 00110100_2$.
    \item \textbf{Iteration 1}: $x - 1 = 51 = 00110011_2 \implies 00110100_2 \mathbin{\&} 00110011_2 = 00110000_2 = 48$ (bit 2 cleared; $\text{count} = 1$).
    \item \textbf{Iteration 2}: $x - 1 = 47 = 00101111_2 \implies 00110000_2 \mathbin{\&} 00101111_2 = 00100000_2 = 32$ (bit 4 cleared; $\text{count} = 2$).
    \item \textbf{Iteration 3}: $x - 1 = 31 = 00011111_2 \implies 00100000_2 \mathbin{\&} 00011111_2 = 00000000_2 = 0$ (bit 5 cleared; $\text{count} = 3$).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Result:} $\text{count} = \mathbf{3}$ in strictly $3$ iterations (positions 2, 4, 5).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Submask Enumeration Trace on \texorpdfstring{$11 = 0\text{b}1011$}{11 = 0b1011}]
Trace $\text{sub} = (\text{sub} - 1) \mathbin{\&} \text{mask}$ for $\text{mask} = 11 = 1011_2$ (set bits $\{0, 1, 3\}$):
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt]
    \item Start: $\text{sub} = 1011_2 = 11$.
    \item $(1011_2 - 1) \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 1010_2 \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 1010_2 = 10$.
    \item $(1010_2 - 1) \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 1001_2 \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 1001_2 = 9$.
    \item $(1001_2 - 1) \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 1000_2 \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 1000_2 = 8$.
    \item $(1000_2 - 1) \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 0111_2 \mathbin{\&} 1011_2 = 0011_2 = 3$ (jumps past $7, 6, 5, 4$!).
    \item Continue: $0010_2 = 2 \to 0001_2 = 1 \to 0000_2 = 0$ (stop).
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Result:} Exact descending sequence $[11, 10, 9, 8, 3, 2, 1, 0]$ ($2^3 = 8$ valid submasks).
\end{example}

\begin{example}[Column Ledger Bit Redistribution for Maximum Sum of Squares]
Given $A = [2, 6, 5, 8]$, you may repeatedly replace any pair $(A[i], A[j])$ with $(A[i] \mathbin{\&} A[j],\; A[i] \mid A[j])$. Choose $k = 2$ elements to maximize their sum of squares.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt]
    \item Write each value in 4-bit binary: $2 = 0010_2$, $6 = 0110_2$, $5 = 0101_2$, $8 = 1000_2$.
    \item Count $1$-bits per column: $\text{count}[0] = 1$ (from $5$), $\text{count}[1] = 2$ (from $2, 6$), $\text{count}[2] = 2$ (from $6, 5$), $\text{count}[3] = 1$ (from $8$).
    \item Because $x^2 + y^2$ is strictly convex, pack as many available $1$-bits as possible into $v_1$ first:
    \begin{itemize}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=1pt]
        \item $v_1$ takes one bit from columns $0, 1, 2, 3$: $v_1 = 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 = 15$ (remaining counts: $[0, 1, 1, 0]$).
        \item $v_2$ takes the remaining bits from columns $1, 2$: $v_2 = 2 + 4 = 6$ (remaining counts: $[0, 0, 0, 0]$).
    \end{itemize}
    \item Compute sum of squares: $v_1^2 + v_2^2 = 15^2 + 6^2 = 225 + 36 = \mathbf{261}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{example}

\subsection{Fundamental Bit Utilities (\texttt{<bit>} + Unsigned Safety)}

Every helper constrains \texttt{T} to \texttt{std::unsigned\_integral}. In \texttt{lowest\_set\_bit}, computing \texttt{val \& (\textasciitilde val + T\{1\})} is UB-free for every unsigned value (returning $0$ for $0$), while \texttt{bit\_width} and \texttt{countr\_zero} map straight to hardware instructions.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={UB-free C++23 bit utilities wrapping \texttt{<bit>} and unsigned bitwise primitives}]
#include <bit>
#include <concepts>
#include <cstdint>

namespace bit_utils {

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr bool test_bit(T val, unsigned int k) noexcept {
    return ((val >> k) & T{1}) != T{0};
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr T set_bit(T val, unsigned int k) noexcept {
    return val | (T{1} << k);
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr T clear_bit(T val, unsigned int k) noexcept {
    return val & ~(T{1} << k);
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr T toggle_bit(T val, unsigned int k) noexcept {
    return val ^ (T{1} << k);
}

// Unsigned-only: avoids signed INT_MIN negation UB; returns 0 for val == 0
template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr T lowest_set_bit(T val) noexcept {
    return val & static_cast<T>(~val + T{1});
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr T clear_lowest_set_bit(T val) noexcept {
    return val & static_cast<T>(val - T{1});
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr int popcount(T val) noexcept {
    return std::popcount(val);
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr bool is_power_of_two(T val) noexcept {
    return std::has_single_bit(val);
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr int lowest_bit_index(T val) noexcept {
    return std::countr_zero(val); // Returns bit width if val == 0
}

template <std::unsigned_integral T>
[[nodiscard]] constexpr int highest_bit_index(T val) noexcept {
    return std::bit_width(val) - 1; // Returns -1 if val == 0
}

} // namespace bit_utils
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Static Prefix XOR Range Query Engine}

The constructor precomputes \texttt{prefix\_[i + 1] = prefix\_[i] \textasciicircum{} arr[i]} in $O(N)$ time, storing a sentinel $0$ at index $0$. Each \texttt{query(left, right)} then XORs \texttt{prefix\_[left]} with \texttt{prefix\_[right + 1]} in $O(1)$ time, canceling elements $0 \dots \text{left} - 1$.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Static Prefix XOR array supporting $O(N)$ build and $O(1)$ range queries}]
#include <cstddef>
#include <cstdint>
#include <span>
#include <vector>

class PrefixXorQueryEngine {
public:
    explicit PrefixXorQueryEngine(std::span<const std::uint32_t> arr)
        : prefix_(arr.size() + 1, 0)
    {
        for (std::size_t i = 0; i < arr.size(); ++i) {
            prefix_[i + 1] = prefix_[i] ^ arr[i];
        }
    }

    // Cumulative XOR of closed subarray [left, right] in O(1); returns 0 if invalid
    [[nodiscard]] std::uint32_t query(std::size_t left, std::size_t right) const noexcept {
        if (left > right || right + 1 >= prefix_.size()) return 0;
        return prefix_[left] ^ prefix_[right + 1];
    }

private:
    std::vector<std::uint32_t> prefix_;
};
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Submask Enumeration and Gosper's Hack (Zero-Overhead Callables)}

Using a C++20/23 \texttt{std::invocable} abbreviated function template lets the compiler inline the callback directly (zero heap allocation unlike \texttt{std::function}). Notice how \texttt{for\_each\_k\_combination} guards $k = 0$, $k > n$, and $n = 64$ safely, and replaces division \texttt{/ c} with \texttt{>> std::countr\_zero(set)}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Zero-overhead submask enumeration and 64-bit-safe Gosper's Hack}]
#include <bit>
#include <concepts>
#include <cstdint>

// Visits every submask of 'mask' (including 0) in strictly descending order
void for_each_submask(std::uint32_t mask,
                      std::invocable<std::uint32_t> auto&& action) {
    for (std::uint32_t sub = mask; ; sub = (sub - 1U) & mask) {
        action(sub);
        if (sub == 0U) break; // Stop after empty submask to prevent unsigned wrap
    }
}

// Visits every n-bit mask with exactly k set bits (0 <= k <= n <= 64)
void for_each_k_combination(unsigned int n, unsigned int k,
                            std::invocable<std::uint64_t> auto&& action) {
    if (k > n || n > 64U) return;
    if (k == 0U) {
        action(0ULL);
        return;
    }
    if (k == 64U) {
        action(~0ULL);
        return;
    }

    std::uint64_t set = (1ULL << k) - 1ULL;
    const std::uint64_t limit = (n == 64U) ? 0ULL : (1ULL << n);

    while (true) {
        action(set);
        const std::uint64_t c = set & (~set + 1ULL);
        const std::uint64_t r = set + c;
        if (r == 0ULL || (n < 64U && r >= limit)) break; // Wrapped or reached 2^n
        set = (((r ^ set) >> 2U) >> std::countr_zero(set)) | r;
    }
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Travelling Salesperson Problem via Bitmask DP}

Starting from city $0$ (\texttt{mask = 1}), we iterate through all $2^N$ subset masks and relax transitions to each unvisited city $v$. Both \texttt{dp[mask][u] == INF} and \texttt{cost[u][v] == INF} are checked before addition so \texttt{INF + INF} can never overflow, and edge cases $N = 0$ and $N = 1$ return $0$ immediately.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Bitmask DP Travelling Salesperson solver in $O(N^2 \cdot 2^N)$ time}]
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstddef>
#include <limits>
#include <vector>

class TravellingSalesperson {
public:
    static constexpr int INF = std::numeric_limits<int>::max() / 2;

    [[nodiscard]] static int solve(const std::vector<std::vector<int>>& cost) {
        const std::size_t n = cost.size();
        if (n <= 1) return 0;

        const std::size_t num_masks = 1ULL << n;
        std::vector<std::vector<int>> dp(num_masks, std::vector<int>(n, INF));
        dp[1][0] = 0; // Base case: visited {0}, standing at city 0

        for (std::size_t mask = 1; mask < num_masks; ++mask) {
            for (std::size_t u = 0; u < n; ++u) {
                if (dp[mask][u] == INF) continue;
                for (std::size_t v = 0; v < n; ++v) {
                    if ((mask & (1ULL << v)) != 0 || cost[u][v] == INF) continue;
                    const std::size_t next_mask = mask | (1ULL << v);
                    dp[next_mask][v] = std::min(dp[next_mask][v], dp[mask][u] + cost[u][v]);
                }
            }
        }

        int min_tour = INF;
        const std::size_t full_mask = num_masks - 1;
        for (std::size_t u = 1; u < n; ++u) {
            if (dp[full_mask][u] != INF && cost[u][0] != INF) {
                min_tour = std::min(min_tour, dp[full_mask][u] + cost[u][0]);
            }
        }
        return min_tour;
    }
};
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Problem Pattern Recognition}
% ============================================================================

When analyzing a new problem, run this three-step check:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Check $N$}: If $N \le 20$ and you must visit, assign, or partition items, think \textbf{Bitmask DP} ($O(N^2 \cdot 2^N)$) or \textbf{Submask Enumeration} ($O(3^N)$). If $N \le 40$, consider Meet-in-the-Middle on two $2^{N/2}$ halves.
    \item \textbf{Check Parity / Cancellation Language}: Duplicate pairs canceling out points to \textbf{XOR}; static interval XOR sums point to \textbf{Prefix XOR}.
    \item \textbf{Check Bitwise Objective Language}: Optimizing an array under \texttt{\&}, \texttt{|}, or \texttt{\textasciicircum} usually decomposes into $32$ or $64$ independent \textbf{Column Ledger} problems.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Glossary}
% ============================================================================

\subsection*{Core Concepts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bit}: Binary digit ($0$ or $1$), the smallest unit of digital information.
    \item \textbf{Bitmask}: An integer treated as a compact boolean set where bit $i = 1$ means element $i$ is included.
    \item \textbf{Least Significant Bit (LSB)}: Bit index $0$ (weight $2^0 = 1$); determines even/odd parity.
    \item \textbf{Most Significant Bit (MSB)}: Highest bit position; dictates magnitude and, in signed integers, the sign.
    \item \textbf{Two's Complement}: Standard signed integer encoding where the MSB carries weight $-2^{N-1}$ and $-x = (\sim x) + 1$.
    \item \textbf{Self-Inverse (Involution)}: An operator that undoes itself: $x \oplus x = 0$ and $(a \oplus b) \oplus b = a$.
    \item \textbf{Submask}: A bitmask whose $1$-bits are a subset of another mask's $1$-bits.
    \item \textbf{State Compression}: Encoding an exponential subset as an integer bitmask index inside a DP table.
    \item \textbf{Population Count (Popcount)}: Total count of $1$-bits in an integer (\texttt{std::popcount}).
    \item \textbf{Logical vs. Arithmetic Right Shift}: Unsigned right shift fills with $0$s; signed right shift duplicates the sign bit.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Mathematical Symbols}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $\mathbin{\&},\; \mid,\; \oplus,\; \sim$: Bitwise AND, OR, Exclusive OR (XOR), and NOT.
    \item $\ll,\; \gg$: Bitwise left shift and right shift.
    \item $b_k,\; 2^k$: Bit value at 0-based index $k$ and its positional weight.
    \item $P[i]$: Prefix XOR of the first $i$ elements ($P[0] = 0$).
    \item $\in,\; \subseteq,\; \emptyset$: Set membership, subset relation, and empty set.
    \item $\binom{N}{K},\; \sum,\; \lfloor \cdot \rfloor$: Binomial coefficient, summation, and floor function.
\end{itemize}

\begin{tryitbox}{Triage}{Cold-Start Bit Pattern Selection}{ex9}

\begin{tryitbox}{Trace}{Prefix XOR Build and Queries}{ex13}

\end{tryitbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Derive}{Why Bit Migration Never Shrinks Sum of Squares}{ex14}
Take $A = 6$ ($110_2$) and $B = 5$ ($101_2$). Compute $A' = A \mathbin{\&} B$ and $B' = A \mid B$.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item Verify that $A' + B' = A + B$, while $(A')^2 + (B')^2 > A^2 + B^2$.
    \item Let $d = A - A' = B' - B \ge 0$ (the value of the migrated bits). Expand $(A - d)^2 + (B + d)^2 - (A^2 + B^2)$ assuming $B \ge A$ (or $A' \le A \le B'$) to see why concentrating bits strictly increases sum of squares!
\end{enumerate}
\end{tryitbox}

\section*{Solutions to Pen-and-Paper Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Solutions to Pen-and-Paper Exercises}
\markboth{Solutions to Pen-and-Paper Exercises}{}
% ============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, parsep=1pt]
    \item \label{sol:ex1}\textbf{Exercise 1 (Trace) --- Two's Complement and LSB Isolation:}
    $+13 = 00001101_2 \to \sim 13 = 11110010_2 \to +1 = \mathbf{11110011_2}$ (check: $-128 + 64 + 32 + 16 + 2 + 1 = -13$).
    For $x = 12 = 00001100_2$: $-x = 11110100_2$, so $x \mathbin{\&} (-x) = 00000100_2 = \mathbf{4}$.
    \trapnote{Forgetting the $+1$ step turns two's complement into one's complement ($11110010_2 = -14$, off by $1$).}

    \item \label{sol:ex2}\textbf{Exercise 2 (Trace) --- Kernighan Stripping and Power-of-Two Edge Case:}
    $42\,(00101010_2) \to 40\,(00101000_2) \to 32\,(00100000_2) \to 0$ ($3$ steps, popcount $= 3$).
    When $x = 0$, $0 \mathbin{\&} (-1) = 0$, so without $x > 0$ (or \texttt{std::has\_single\_bit}), zero falsely passes as a power of two!
    \trapnote{Omitting the \texttt{x > 0} guard is the \#1 power-of-two interview bug---prefer C++20 \texttt{std::has\_single\_bit(x)}.}

    \item \label{sol:ex3}\textbf{Exercise 3 (Edge Cases) --- C++23 Bit Header Outputs:}
    For $0$: \texttt{countl\_zero}$=8$, \texttt{countr\_zero}$=8$, \texttt{bit\_width}$=0$, \texttt{bit\_floor}$=0$, \texttt{bit\_ceil}$=1$.
    For $1$: $7, 0, 1, 1, 1$. For $12\,(00001100_2)$: \texttt{countl\_zero}$=4$, \texttt{countr\_zero}$=2$, \texttt{bit\_width}$=4$, \texttt{bit\_floor}$=8$, \texttt{bit\_ceil}$=16$.
    \trapnote{Passing a signed \texttt{int} to \texttt{<bit>} functions fails to compile, and \texttt{bit\_width(0)} is $0$ (so \texttt{bit\_width(0) - 1} underflows if stored in an unsigned type!).}

    \item \label{sol:ex4}\textbf{Exercise 4 (Trace) --- Two Unique Numbers via XOR Partitioning:}
    $X = 6 \oplus 3 = 110_2 \oplus 011_2 = 101_2 = 5$. $\text{diff} = 5 \mathbin{\&} (-5) = 1$ (bit $0$).
    Group~0 (even, bit $0$ is $0$): $[4, 2, 4, 6, 2] \implies \text{XOR} = \mathbf{6}$. Group~1 (odd, bit $0$ is $1$): $[3] \implies \text{XOR} = \mathbf{3}$.
    \trapnote{Partitioning with \texttt{x \& X} instead of a single isolated bit \texttt{x \& diff} can put both unique numbers into the non-zero group!}

    \item \label{sol:ex5}\textbf{Exercise 5 (Trace) --- Submask Enumeration of 13:}
    $13\,(1101) \to 12\,(1100) \to 9\,(1001) \to 8\,(1000) \to 5\,(0101) \to 4\,(0100) \to 1\,(0001) \to 0\,(0000)$.
    Skipped non-submasks: $11, 10, 7, 6, 3, 2$ (every number having bit $1$ set).
    \trapnote{Stopping at \texttt{sub > 0} skips the empty submask $0$---if empty subsets matter in your DP, break \emph{after} processing \texttt{sub == 0}.}

    \item \label{sol:ex6}\textbf{Exercise 6 (Derive) --- Verify the 3-to-the-N Pair Count:}
    Pairs: $(00,00)$ [1]; $(01,01),(01,00)$ [2]; $(10,10),(10,00)$ [2]; $(11,11),(11,10),(11,01),(11,00)$ [4]. Total $= 1 + 2 + 2 + 4 = \mathbf{9} = 3^2$.
    Each bit can be $(0,0)$, $(1,0)$, or $(1,1)$---never $(0,1)$---giving $3$ independent choices per bit.
    \trapnote{Confusing $O(3^N)$ with the cost for a \emph{single} mask: a single mask with $k$ set bits has $2^k$ submasks; $3^N$ is the sum across all $2^N$ masks.}

    \item \label{sol:ex7}\textbf{Exercise 7 (Trace) --- Two Steps of Gosper's Hack:}
    Step 1 ($\text{set} = 00111_2 = 7$): $c = 1$, $r = 01000_2 = 8$, $r \oplus \text{set} = 01111_2$, shifted right by $2 + 0 = 2$ gives $00011_2$, OR $r = \mathbf{01011_2 = 11}$.
    Step 2 ($\text{set} = 01011_2 = 11$): $c = 1$, $r = 01100_2 = 12$, $r \oplus \text{set} = 00111_2$, shifted right by $2 + 0 = 2$ gives $00001_2$, OR $r = \mathbf{01101_2 = 13}$.
    \trapnote{Forgetting the $+2$ shift (or dividing by $c$ instead of shifting by \texttt{countr\_zero(set)}) leaves too many $1$-bits in the next mask.}

    \item \label{sol:ex8}\textbf{Exercise 8 (Debug) --- Spot Two Classic C++ Bit Bugs:}
    (1) \texttt{1} is a 32-bit signed \texttt{int}; shifting by $35 \ge 32$ is UB \emph{before} conversion to \texttt{uint64\_t}. Fix: \texttt{1ULL << 35}.
    (2) Negating signed \texttt{INT\_MIN} overflows $+2^{31}$ (UB). Fix: accept \texttt{std::unsigned\_integral} and use \texttt{val \& (\textasciitilde val + T\{1\})}.
    \trapnote{Assigning \texttt{1 << 35} to a \texttt{uint64\_t} does \emph{not} make the literal 64-bit---the shift happens first in 32-bit \texttt{int}.}

    \item \label{sol:ex9}\textbf{Exercise 9 (Triage) --- Cold-Start Bit Pattern Selection:}
    \emph{A}: \textbf{Bitmask DP} ($O(N \cdot 2^N)$: assign worker $i = \text{popcount(mask)}$ to any unassigned job bit).
    \emph{B}: \textbf{Prefix XOR} ($O(N)$ build, $O(1)$ per query).
    \emph{C}: \textbf{Column Ledger mod 3} ($O(32 \cdot N)$ time, $O(1)$ space).
    \trapnote{Adding an extra worker dimension to Scenario~A gives $O(N^2 \cdot 2^N)$, which is $18\times$ slower than deriving the worker index directly from \texttt{std::popcount(mask)}.}

    \item \label{sol:ex10}\textbf{Exercise 10 (Twist) --- Single Element Among Triples:}
    XOR cancels \emph{even} counts, so $5 \oplus 5 \oplus 5 = 5$ leaves $5 \oplus 2 = 7$ instead of $2$.
    Binary: $5 = 101_2$ ($3\times$), $2 = 010_2$ ($1\times$). Column counts: $\text{count}[0] = 3$, $\text{count}[1] = 1$, $\text{count}[2] = 3$. Taking each column $\bmod 3$ gives $[0, 1, 0] \implies 010_2 = \mathbf{2}$.
    \trapnote{Trying to use XOR for odd multiplicities ($3\times, 5\times$): XOR is strictly mod $2$; for mod $k$, use a 32-slot column counter mod $k$.}

    \item \label{sol:ex11}\textbf{Exercise 11 (Debug) --- Two Infinite-Loop and Overflow Bugs:}
    (1) \texttt{uint32\_t sub >= 0} is \textbf{always true}! After \texttt{sub == 0}, \texttt{(0 - 1) \& mask} wraps to \texttt{mask}, looping forever.
    (2) When \texttt{dp[mask][u]} is large and \texttt{cost[u][v] == INF}, adding them overflows signed 32-bit \texttt{int} into a huge \emph{negative} number, corrupting \texttt{std::min}!
    \trapnote{Using \texttt{>= 0} on unsigned loop counters is one of the most notorious C++ infinite loops.}

    \item \label{sol:ex12}\textbf{Exercise 12 (Edge Cases) --- Four Bitwise Boundary Checks:}
    (1) \texttt{false} and \texttt{0U}.
    (2) \texttt{P = \{0, 7, 4, 1\}}, so \texttt{query(0, 0)} $= P[0] \oplus P[1] = 0 \oplus 7 = \mathbf{7}$.
    (3) Runs \textbf{once} with mask \texttt{0ULL} ($\binom{4}{0} = 1$, the empty subset).
    (4) Returns \textbf{0} immediately via \texttt{if (n <= 1) return 0;}.
    \trapnote{Forgetting that $\binom{N}{0} = 1$ (not $0$): there is always one 0-element subset---the empty mask \texttt{0ULL}.}

    \item \label{sol:ex13}\textbf{Exercise 13 (Trace) --- Prefix XOR Build and Queries:}
    $P = [0,\; 3,\; 3\oplus 1 = 2,\; 2\oplus 4 = 6,\; 6\oplus 6 = 0,\; 0\oplus 2 = 2]$.
    \texttt{range\_xor(1, 3)} $= P[1] \oplus P[4] = 3 \oplus 0 = \mathbf{3}$ (direct: $1 \oplus 4 \oplus 6 = 3$).
    \texttt{range\_xor(2, 4)} $= P[2] \oplus P[5] = 2 \oplus 2 = \mathbf{0}$ (direct: $4 \oplus 6 \oplus 2 = 0$).
    \trapnote{Indexing $P[R]$ instead of $P[R + 1]$ drops the right endpoint $A[R]$ from the subarray XOR.}

    \item \label{sol:ex14}\textbf{Exercise 14 (Derive) --- Why Bit Migration Never Shrinks Sum of Squares:}
    (1) $A' = 6 \mathbin{\&} 5 = 4$, $B' = 6 \mid 5 = 7$. Sums: $4 + 7 = 11 = 6 + 5$. Squares: $4^2 + 7^2 = 65 > 6^2 + 5^2 = 61$.
    (2) Writing $(A', B') = (A - d, B + d)$ when bits of value $d$ move from $A$ to $B$ (with $B \ge A$): $(A - d)^2 + (B + d)^2 - (A^2 + B^2) = 2d(B - A + d) \ge 0$ (strictly positive whenever $d > 0$)!
    \trapnote{Assuming $A \mathbin{\&} B \le A \le B \le A \mid B$ changes the sum---in fact $(A \mathbin{\&} B) + (A \mid B) = A + B$ is an exact identity for all integers.}
\end{enumerate}
