\begin{overviewbox}[What You'll Learn]
Greedy algorithms build a solution one irreversible step at a time, always picking whatever looks best right now without backtracking or exploring all branches. When a problem supports it, this drops exponential $O(2^N)$ or quadratic $O(N^2)$ searches down to a blazing $O(N \log N)$ sort or $O(N)$ linear pass---but when the structure does not support it, greedy silently returns the wrong answer. You will learn how to \emph{prove} a greedy rule works (Exchange Argument and Greedy Stays Ahead), spot Dynamic Programming traps, and master five patterns: Interval Scheduling/Partitioning, Fractional Knapsack, Huffman/Rope Merging, Two-Pointer Matching, and Furthest-Reach Horizon Tracking.
\end{overviewbox}

\section{Foundations and Core Concepts}
% ============================================================================

Imagine you are a cashier handing back $41$ cents in US coins ($1, 5, 10, 25$). You do not build a search tree of every coin combination. You immediately grab the largest coin that fits ($25$), then $10$, then $5$, then $1$, and never reconsider an earlier coin.

A \textbf{greedy algorithm} works the exact same way. It builds an answer piece by piece, always committing to the single choice that looks best at the current step. Once a decision is made, it is never undone.

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Foundations]
$N$ & Number of input items, intervals, or elements & $N = 10^5$ (\texttt{std::size\_t}) \\
$G$ & The sequence of choices produced by our greedy rule & $G = (g_1, g_2, \dots, g_k)$ \\
$O^*$ & Any valid globally optimal solution (used in proofs) & $O^* = (o_1, o_2, \dots, o_m)$ \\
$O(N \log N)$ & Typical runtime when dominated by sorting or a heap & $\approx 1.7 \times 10^6$ ops at $N = 10^5$ \\
\end{notationbox}

For a shortsighted rule to guarantee a globally best answer, a problem must satisfy two structural properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Greedy Choice Property}: There exists at least one globally optimal solution that begins with your locally best choice. You never have to ``sacrifice'' the current step to unlock a better future.
    \item \textbf{Optimal Substructure}: After you commit to that greedy choice, the leftover problem is a smaller, independent instance of the exact same problem.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{But Why Does Greedy Work? --- Two Proof Recipes}

\painbeat The hardest part of greedy algorithms is not writing the code---the code is often 10 lines long. The danger is inventing a plausible-sounding sorting rule that passes your first two test cases and silently fails on the third.

\ahabeat You do not need formal academic math to trust a greedy rule. Almost every greedy algorithm in programming is proven by one of two concrete, mechanical recipes: the \textbf{Exchange Argument} or \textbf{Greedy Stays Ahead}.

\textbf{Recipe 1: The Exchange Argument (The ``Swap Test'').}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Assume some globally optimal solution $O^*$ exists, and compare it with your greedy solution $G$.
    \item Find the \emph{first} step where $O^*$ picked a different choice ($o_1$) instead of your greedy choice ($g_1$).
    \item \textbf{Swap} $o_1$ out of $O^*$ and plug $g_1$ in, creating a modified solution $O'$.
    \item Show two things: (a) $O'$ is still \emph{valid} (no new conflicts or constraint violations), and (b) $O'$ is \emph{at least as good} as $O^*$ (same or better value/count/cost).
\end{enumerate}

If every difference can be swapped away without hurting optimality, then $G$ is guaranteed to be just as good as $O^*$!

\textbf{Recipe 2: Greedy Stays Ahead.}
Track a single scoreboard metric after step $k$ (for example, the finish time of the $k$-th chosen interval, or the furthest index reached after $k$ jumps). Prove by induction that after every step $k$, your greedy solution's scoreboard is \emph{at least as favorable} as any optimal solution's scoreboard at step $k$.

\subsection{When Greedy Fails: Greedy vs. Dynamic Programming}

Never assume greedy works just because a problem asks for a minimum or maximum! Before coding a greedy rule, spend 30 seconds actively trying to \textbf{break it with a 3-element counterexample}.

\examplebeat Consider two famous greedy traps:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Non-Canonical Coin Change}: Given coin denominations $\{1, 3, 4\}$ and target amount $6$, the largest-coin-first greedy rule takes $4$, leaving $2$, forcing $1 + 1$ ($3$ coins total). Dynamic Programming tests every coin and finds $3 + 3$ ($2$ coins).
    \item \textbf{0/1 Knapsack (Indivisible Items)}: With capacity $W = 50$ and items $A(v{=}60, w{=}10)$, $B(v{=}100, w{=}20)$, $C(v{=}120, w{=}30)$, sorting by value/weight density ($6, 5, 4$) greedily picks $A + B$ for value $160$ and wastes $20\text{ kg}$ of capacity. Picking $B + C$ fills all $50\text{ kg}$ for value $220$!
\end{itemize}

Every major greedy problem falls into one of five core templates. Read each subsection's 4-beat origin story to see \emph{why} the specific sorting key or heap rule works---and exactly where edge cases bite.

\subsection{Interval Scheduling and Interval Partitioning}

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Interval Problems]
$[s_i, f_i)$ & Half-open event starting at $s_i$ and ending at $f_i$ & $[1, 4)$ and $[4, 7)$ do \emph{not} overlap \\
$f_{\text{last}}$ & Finish time of the most recently accepted event & Initialized to $-\infty$ (\texttt{INT\_MIN}) \\
$K$ & Minimum rooms (partitions) needed to host \emph{all} events & Equals peak overlap depth \\
\end{notationbox}

\textbf{Part A: Interval Scheduling (Maximum Non-Overlapping Events).}

\painbeat Given $N$ events $[s_i, f_i)$ competing for a single conference room, trying every compatible subset takes $O(2^N)$ time. Worse, intuitive rules like ``earliest start'' or ``shortest meeting'' fail on simple 3-event inputs (as you saw in Exercise~1).

\ahabeat Only one thing determines how much room is left for future events: \textbf{how soon the room becomes free again}. Picking the event with the \textbf{earliest finish time} $f_i$ leaves the largest possible suffix of the timeline open for everything else.

\examplebeat Consider four events: $A=[1, 4)$, $B=[2, 6)$, $C=[4, 7)$, $D=[5, 9)$. Sorting by finish time gives $A, B, C, D$. Pick $A$ ($f_{\text{last}} = 4$). Skip $B$ because $2 < 4$. Pick $C$ because $4 \ge 4$ ($f_{\text{last}} = 7$). Skip $D$ because $5 < 7$. Maximum events: $2$ ($\{A, C\}$).

\invariantbeat{Interval Scheduling} After picking $k$ intervals by earliest finish time, our $k$-th finish time is $\le$ the $k$-th finish time of \emph{any} valid schedule of size $k$ (Greedy Stays Ahead).

\begin{watchoutbox}[Watch Out --- Touching Endpoints and Negative Timestamps]
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Touching endpoints}: Clarify whether $[1, 4]$ and $[4, 7]$ overlap. For standard half-open events (a meeting ending at 4:00 frees the room for a 4:00 meeting), check \texttt{ev.start >= current\_end}. If closed endpoints conflict, use \texttt{ev.start > current\_end}.
    \item \textbf{Sentinel bug}: Initializing \texttt{current\_end = -1} breaks if coordinates can be negative (e.g., $[-10, -5]$). Always initialize to \texttt{std::numeric\_limits<int>::min()} (or track a \texttt{std::optional<int>}).
\end{itemize}
\end{watchoutbox}

\textbf{Part B: Interval Partitioning (Minimum Meeting Rooms).}

\painbeat Now flip the goal: you must host \emph{every} event simultaneously using as few rooms $K$ as possible. Sorting by finish time no longer tells you which room is free when a new meeting starts!

\ahabeat Process events in chronological order of \textbf{start time} $s_i$, and keep a \textbf{min-heap} of the finish times of all currently active rooms. When event $i$ arrives, look only at the room that finishes \emph{earliest} (the heap top): if $\text{top} \le s_i$, reuse that room (pop old finish, push $f_i$); otherwise, every active room is busy, so you are forced to open a new room (push $f_i$).

\begin{formulabox}[Minimum Meeting Rooms Formula]
$$K_{\min} = \max_{t \in \mathbb{R}} \big|\{\, i : s_i \le t < f_i \,\}\big|$$
\textbf{Read this as:} The minimum number of rooms equals the \emph{maximum number of meetings overlapping at the same instant} (the peak depth). Because our algorithm opens a new room only when all existing rooms overlap with the incoming meeting at time $s_i$, it never allocates more rooms than this unavoidable lower bound.
\end{formulabox}

\examplebeat Meetings $[0, 30), [5, 10), [15, 20)$ sorted by start time:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item $[0, 30)$: heap empty $\to$ open Room 1. Heap: $\{30\}$.
    \item $[5, 10)$: earliest free is $30 > 5$ $\to$ open Room 2. Heap: $\{10, 30\}$.
    \item $[15, 20)$: earliest free is $10 \le 15$ $\to$ reuse Room 2! Pop $10$, push $20$. Heap: $\{20, 30\}$.
\end{enumerate}
Final heap size is $2$, so $K_{\min} = 2$ rooms suffice.

\begin{tryitbox}{Trace}{Earliest Finish vs. Minimum Rooms}{ex2}
Given five half-open events $[1, 5), [2, 3), [3, 6), [5, 8), [6, 7)$:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item What is the \textbf{maximum number of non-overlapping events} you can attend in one room? List your chosen set.
    \item What is the \textbf{minimum number of rooms} required to host all five events?
\end{enumerate}
\end{tryitbox}

\subsection{Fractional Knapsack}

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Fractional Knapsack]
$W$ & Total weight capacity of the knapsack & $W = 50.0$ (\texttt{double}) \\
$v_i, w_i$ & Total value and total weight of item $i$ & $v_i \ge 0,\; w_i \ge 0$ \\
$\rho_i$ & Value density (value per single unit of weight) & $\rho_i = v_i / w_i$ \\
$x_i$ & Fraction of item $i$ taken into the knapsack & $x_i \in [0, 1]$ \\
\end{notationbox}

\painbeat When you pack indivisible bars into a backpack (0/1 Knapsack), greedy fails because awkward gaps waste capacity. What if the goods are \textbf{divisible}---like gold dust, coffee beans, or fuel---so you can pour any fraction $x_i \in [0, 1]$?

\ahabeat Think of the knapsack as $W$ tiny $1$-gram slots. Every gram you put into the bag should earn as much value as possible! Therefore, greedily filling slots in descending order of \textbf{value density} $\rho_i = v_i / w_i$ is provably optimal: if any optimal bag left behind even $1\text{ gram}$ of a denser item while carrying $1\text{ gram}$ of a cheaper item, swapping those two grams strictly increases total value (Exchange Argument).

\begin{formulabox}[Fractional Contribution Formula]
$$\rho_i = \frac{v_i}{w_i}, \qquad \Delta V = \begin{cases} v_i & \text{if } w_i \le W_{\text{rem}} \\ v_i \cdot \dfrac{W_{\text{rem}}}{w_i} & \text{if } w_i > W_{\text{rem}} \end{cases}$$
\textbf{Read this as:} Sort items by value-per-kilogram $\rho_i$ from highest to lowest. Take whole items while they fit; for the first item that exceeds remaining capacity $W_{\text{rem}}$, slice off the exact fraction $W_{\text{rem}} / w_i$ to fill the bag to $100\%$, then stop.
\end{formulabox}

\examplebeat Suppose $W = 50\text{ kg}$ with three items: $A(v{=}60, w{=}10, \rho{=}6)$, $B(v{=}100, w{=}20, \rho{=}5)$, and $C(v{=}120, w{=}30, \rho{=}4)$. Take all of $A$ ($+60$, rem $=40$), all of $B$ ($+100$, rem $=20$), and $\frac{20}{30} = \frac{2}{3}$ of $C$ ($+80$, rem $=0$). Total value: $240$ (see Figure~\ref{fig:fractional_knapsack}).

\subsection{Huffman Coding and Minimum-Cost Rope Merging}

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Rope Merging / Huffman]
$L_i$ & Initial length of rope $i$ (or frequency of symbol $i$) & $L_i > 0$ (\texttt{std::int64\_t}) \\
$d_i$ & Merge depth of item $i$ (number of merges it participates in) & Leaf depth in merge tree \\
$H$ & Min-heap storing all active rope lengths / subtree weights & \texttt{std::priority\_queue} \\
\end{notationbox}

\painbeat You have $N$ ropes of lengths $L_1, \dots, L_N$. Connecting two ropes of lengths $a$ and $b$ costs $a + b$ and produces one new rope of length $a + b$. What if you just sort once and merge left-to-right? On $[3, 4, 5, 6]$, linear merging computes $(3{+}4){=}7$, then $(7{+}5){=}12$, then $(12{+}6){=}18$, for total cost $7 + 12 + 18 = 37$---which is \emph{not} optimal!

\ahabeat Every time a rope is part of a merge, its original length gets paid \emph{again}. If original rope $L_i$ sits at depth $d_i$ in the binary merge tree, it contributes $L_i \cdot d_i$ to the total cost. By the Exchange Argument, the two \textbf{smallest} remaining pieces must be merged deepest (first)---and because a newly merged rope can be \emph{larger} than other unmerged ropes, we re-insert it into a \textbf{min-heap} so we always extract the two smallest \emph{current} pieces.

\begin{formulabox}[Weighted Path Length Identity (Huffman / Rope Cost)]
$$\text{Total Merge Cost} = \sum_{\text{all merges}} (a + b) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} L_i \cdot d_i$$
\textbf{Read this as:} Summing every internal merge cost gives the exact same number as multiplying each original rope length (or character frequency in Huffman compression) by how many merges it lived through. Minimizing cost means pushing heavy items toward the top (small $d_i$) and light items to the bottom (large $d_i$).
\end{formulabox}

\examplebeat Re-run $[3, 4, 5, 6]$ with a min-heap:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item Pop $3, 4 \implies$ merge cost $7$, push $7$. Heap: $\{5, 6, 7\}$.
    \item Pop $5, 6 \implies$ merge cost $11$, push $11$. Heap: $\{7, 11\}$.
    \item Pop $7, 11 \implies$ merge cost $18$, push $18$. Heap: $\{18\}$ (done!).
\end{enumerate}
Total cost $= 7 + 11 + 18 = \mathbf{36}$ (beating linear merging's $37$!). Notice that every single leaf sat at depth $2$, so $3(2) + 4(2) + 5(2) + 6(2) = 36$.

\begin{watchoutbox}[Watch Out --- Single Rope and 32-Bit Overflow]
If $N \le 1$, zero merges are needed, so the cost is \textbf{0} (not $L_0$!). Also, merging $N = 10^5$ ropes of length $10^5$ produces sums up to $10^{10}$, overflowing 32-bit \texttt{int}. Always accumulate both heap elements and total cost in \texttt{std::int64\_t}.
\end{watchoutbox}

\subsection{Two-Pointer Greedy Matching}

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Two-Pointer Matching]
$D[0 \dots m{-}1]$ & Sorted array of demands (e.g., task difficulties or hunger) & Ascending order \\
$S[0 \dots n{-}1]$ & Sorted array of supplies (e.g., worker strengths or cookies) & Ascending order \\
$i, j$ & Current demand pointer ($i$) and supply pointer ($j$) & Each advances $O(1)$ per step \\
\end{notationbox}

\painbeat Suppose you have $m$ tasks requiring minimum skill $D[i]$ and $n$ workers with skill $S[j]$, where each worker can take at most one task. Checking every worker-task assignment is a bipartite matching problem; even naive greedy matching without sorting wastes a level-$10$ worker on a level-$1$ task.

\ahabeat Sort both arrays in ascending order. Try to satisfy the \emph{easiest remaining task} $D[i]$ using the \emph{weakest worker} $S[j]$ who can handle it ($S[j] \ge D[i]$). If $S[j] < D[i]$, worker $j$ cannot handle task $i$---nor any harder task $D[k > i]$---so discarding worker $j$ loses nothing.

\examplebeat Let tasks be $D = [2, 4, 6]$ and workers be $S = [1, 3, 5]$ (already sorted):
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item $i{=}0, j{=}0$: $S[0]{=}1 < D[0]{=}2$ $\to$ worker $1$ is too weak for everyone; advance $j \to 1$.
    \item $i{=}0, j{=}1$: $S[1]{=}3 \ge D[0]{=}2$ $\to$ match! Advance both: $i \to 1, j \to 2$.
    \item $i{=}1, j{=}2$: $S[2]{=}5 \ge D[1]{=}4$ $\to$ match! Advance both: $i \to 2, j \to 3$ (out of workers).
\end{enumerate}
Total matched tasks: $\mathbf{2}$.

\invariantbeat{Two-Pointer Matching} Every worker before index $j$ is either already matched to a task before $i$, or too weak for task $i$ (and therefore too weak for all remaining tasks).

\begin{watchoutbox}[Watch Out --- Advancing the Wrong Pointer]
When $S[j] < D[i]$, advance \textbf{only} the supply pointer $j$---never advance the demand pointer $i$! A stronger worker later in $S$ may still satisfy task $D[i]$. Also remember that both arrays must be sorted before scanning.
\end{watchoutbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Twist}{Boats to Save People}{ex5}
People with weights $[3, 2, 2, 1]$ must cross a river in boats of weight limit $3$. Each boat carries \textbf{at most two people}. If you sort weights ascending, should you pair the heaviest person with the lightest person (opposite-direction two pointers) or pair adjacent light people? \end{tryitbox}

\subsection{Furthest-Reach Greedy (Jump Horizon Tracking)}

\begin{notationbox}[Symbols Used in Horizon Tracking]
$A[i]$ & Maximum forward jump length allowed from index $i$ & $A[i] \ge 0$ \\
$R_{\max}$ & Furthest index reachable from any position scanned so far & $R_{\max} = \max_{k \le i}(k + A[k])$ \\
$E_{\text{cur}}$ & Right boundary of the current jump layer (for min jumps) & Triggers \texttt{jumps++} when $i = E_{\text{cur}}$ \\
\end{notationbox}

\painbeat Given an array $A$ where $A[i]$ is the maximum jump length from index $i$, can you reach index $N - 1$---and in how few jumps? Dynamic Programming checks every previous index $j < i$ that can reach $i$, taking $O(N^2)$ time and timing out when $N = 10^5$.

\ahabeat You never need to decide \emph{which} landing square inside your current jump range you picked until you reach the edge of that range! Think of each jump as expanding a BFS wave across the array: while scanning $i$ from $0$ up to the current wave boundary $E_{\text{cur}}$, simply track the single furthest horizon $R_{\max} = \max(R_{\max}, i + A[i])$ reachable in the \emph{next} jump.

\begin{formulabox}[Horizon Expansion Invariant]
$$\text{Reachable if } i \le R_{\max} \text{ for all } i, \quad \text{updating } R_{\max} \leftarrow \max(R_{\max},\; i + A[i]).$$
\textbf{Read this as:} As long as your walking index $i$ never steps past the furthest horizon $R_{\max}$ unlocked by earlier squares, square $i$ is guaranteed to be reachable. Every time $i$ reaches the edge $E_{\text{cur}}$ of the current jump, spend $+1$ jump and push the edge out to $E_{\text{cur}} \leftarrow R_{\max}$.
\end{formulabox}

\examplebeat On $A = [2, 3, 1, 1, 4]$ (target index $4$), start with $E_{\text{cur}} = 0$, $R_{\max} = 0$, $\text{jumps} = 0$:
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item $i = 0$: $R_{\max} = \max(0, 0{+}2) = 2$. Hit $i == E_{\text{cur}}$ $\to$ $\text{jumps} = 1$, $E_{\text{cur}} = 2$.
    \item $i = 1$: $R_{\max} = \max(2, 1{+}3) = 4$. (Still inside jump~1's boundary $E_{\text{cur}} = 2$.)
    \item $i = 2$: $R_{\max} = \max(4, 2{+}1) = 4$. Hit $i == E_{\text{cur}}$ $\to$ $\text{jumps} = 2$, $E_{\text{cur}} = 4 \ge N{-}1$ (done!).
\end{itemize}
Contrast with $A = [3, 2, 1, 0, 4]$: at $i = 0, 1, 2, 3$, $R_{\max}$ stays stuck at $3$. When $i = 4 > R_{\max} = 3$, we immediately know index $4$ is unreachable.

\begin{watchoutbox}[Watch Out --- Looping to the Final Index]
In the minimum-jumps loop, stop at $i < N - 1$ (not $i < N$)! If $E_{\text{cur}}$ lands \emph{exactly} on $N - 1$, visiting $i = N - 1$ would falsely trigger one extra \texttt{jumps++} after you have already arrived.
\end{watchoutbox}

\section{Key Algorithmic Patterns}
% ============================================================================

Why do brute-force and generic DP lose so badly on greedy-friendly problems? Because they repeatedly re-evaluate choices that are already \emph{dominated} by a single extreme element.

\begingroup\small\rowcolors{2}{codebg}{white}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\raggedright\arraybackslash\bfseries}p{3.4cm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}p{3.5cm} >{\raggedright\arraybackslash}X >{\centering\arraybackslash}p{2.3cm}}
\arrayrulecolor{primaryblue}\toprule[1.2pt]
\rowcolor{primaryblue!10}
\textcolor{primaryblue}{\textbf{Problem Family}} & \textcolor{primaryblue}{\textbf{Naive / DP Approach}} & \textcolor{primaryblue}{\textbf{Greedy Shortcut}} & \textcolor{primaryblue}{\textbf{Speedup}} \\
\arrayrulecolor{codeframe}\midrule[0.6pt]
Interval Scheduling & Subsets $O(2^N)$ / DP $O(N^2)$ & Sort by earliest finish time $f_i$, linear scan & $O(N \log N)$ \\
Min Meeting Rooms & Check all pairs $O(N^2)$ & Sort by start $s_i$ + min-heap of room ends & $O(N \log N)$ \\
Fractional Knapsack & Discretized DP $O(N W)$ & Sort by density $v_i / w_i$, fill until $W = 0$ & $O(N \log N)$ \\
Rope Merging / Huffman & Interval DP $O(N^3)$ & Min-heap: pop smallest two, push sum & $O(N \log N)$ \\
Jump Horizon & Reachability DP $O(N^2)$ & Track single furthest index $R_{\max}$ & $O(N)$ \\
\arrayrulecolor{primaryblue}\bottomrule[1.2pt]
\end{tabularx}\endgroup

Across all competitive and engineering problems, greedy implementations fall into \textbf{three structural archetypes}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Static Sort + Linear Scan} ($O(N \log N)$): Item priorities never change while you scan. Sort once by the greedy key (finish time, density, or size), then scan in a single $O(N)$ pass.
    \item \textbf{Dynamic Extremum via Min-Heap} ($O(N \log N)$): Each step creates new items or updates active resources (merged ropes, freed meeting rooms). A \texttt{std::priority\_queue} keeps the current minimum at the top in $O(\log N)$ per update.
    \item \textbf{Online Horizon / Prefix Surplus} ($O(N)$): Items already arrive in a fixed array order (Jump Game, circular Gas Station tour). Maintain one running invariant ($R_{\max}$ or running tank balance) in $O(1)$ space.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{calloutbox}{The Sorting vs. Heap vs. Horizon Signal}
Ask yourself one question when designing a greedy pass: \textbf{``Does my next best choice ever change after I act?''}
\begin{itemize}[itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{No, fixed order up front} $\implies$ Sort once (\texttt{std::ranges::sort}), scan linearly.
    \item \textbf{Yes, new/modified candidates appear} $\implies$ Maintain a min-heap (\texttt{std::priority\_queue}).
    \item \textbf{Input order is already fixed by index} $\implies$ Track a single $O(1)$ horizon or running balance.
\end{itemize}
\end{calloutbox}

\section{C++23 Implementations}
% ============================================================================

Every listing below is standalone C++23 with explicit headers, \texttt{[[nodiscard]]}, zero \texttt{using namespace std;}, and defensive guards for empty inputs, negative values, overflow, and floating-point NaN edge cases.

\subsection{Interval Scheduling (Earliest Finish Time)}

Using C++20/23 \texttt{std::ranges::sort} with a pointer-to-member \textbf{projection} (\texttt{\&Event::end}) eliminates verbose boilerplate lambdas. Initializing \texttt{current\_end} to \texttt{std::numeric\_limits<int>::min()} handles negative timestamps cleanly; invalid intervals where \texttt{start > end} are skipped.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Interval Scheduling in $O(N \log N)$ time using C++23 projection sorting}]
#include <algorithm>
#include <limits>
#include <vector>

struct Event {
    int start;
    int end;
};

[[nodiscard]] int max_events(std::vector<Event> events) {
    // Greedy choice: sort ascending by earliest finish time via projection
    std::ranges::sort(events, {}, &Event::end);

    int count = 0;
    int current_end = std::numeric_limits<int>::min();

    for (const auto& ev : events) {
        if (ev.start > ev.end) continue; // Ignore malformed intervals
        if (ev.start >= current_end) {
            ++count;
            current_end = ev.end;
        }
    }
    return count;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Interval Partitioning (Minimum Meeting Rooms)}

Here we sort by \emph{start time} (\texttt{\&Event::start}) and keep a min-heap of currently occupied room finish times. Whenever the earliest-finishing room frees up at or before \texttt{ev.start}, we pop it and reuse that room for \texttt{ev.end}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Minimum Meeting Rooms in $O(N \log N)$ time and $O(N)$ space}]
#include <algorithm>
#include <functional>
#include <queue>
#include <vector>

struct Meeting {
    int start;
    int end;
};

[[nodiscard]] int min_meeting_rooms(std::vector<Meeting> meetings) {
    if (meetings.empty()) return 0;

    std::ranges::sort(meetings, {}, &Meeting::start);

    // Min-heap of finish times of currently occupied rooms
    std::priority_queue<int, std::vector<int>, std::greater<>> room_ends;

    for (const auto& m : meetings) {
        if (m.start >= m.end) continue; // Ignore zero-length or invalid intervals
        if (!room_ends.empty() && room_ends.top() <= m.start) {
            room_ends.pop(); // Reuse the room that finished earliest
        }
        room_ends.push(m.end);
    }
    return static_cast<int>(room_ends.size());
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Fractional Knapsack (NaN-Safe Comparator)}

To eliminate the \texttt{0.0 / 0.0} NaN strict-weak-ordering UB, we first partition out non-positive weights: zero-weight items with positive value are collected immediately for free, while non-positive values or negative weights are ignored. Every remaining item has strictly positive weight, making density ratios finite and safe to sort.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={NaN-safe Fractional Knapsack in $O(N \log N)$ time}]
#include <algorithm>
#include <span>
#include <vector>

struct Item {
    double value;
    double weight;
};

[[nodiscard]] double max_knapsack_value(double capacity, std::span<const Item> input) {
    if (capacity <= 0.0) return 0.0;

    double total_value = 0.0;
    std::vector<Item> positive_items;
    positive_items.reserve(input.size());

    // Handle zero-weight items first so division never produces NaN or +Inf
    for (const auto& item : input) {
        if (item.value <= 0.0 || item.weight < 0.0) continue;
        if (item.weight == 0.0) {
            total_value += item.value; // Free value with zero weight cost
        } else {
            positive_items.push_back(item);
        }
    }

    // Strict weak ordering is guaranteed because every weight is strictly > 0
    std::ranges::sort(positive_items, [](const Item& a, const Item& b) {
        return (a.value / a.weight) > (b.value / b.weight);
    });

    for (const auto& item : positive_items) {
        if (capacity >= item.weight) {
            total_value += item.value;
            capacity -= item.weight;
        } else {
            total_value += item.value * (capacity / item.weight);
            break; // Bag is now 100% full
        }
    }
    return total_value;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Huffman / Minimum-Cost Rope Merging}

Constructing the min-heap directly from a \texttt{std::vector} runs bottom-up \texttt{std::make\_heap} in $O(N)$ time, followed by $N - 1$ pairs of pops and one push ($O(N \log N)$ total). All sums use \texttt{std::int64\_t} to prevent overflow.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Minimum-Cost Rope Merging (Huffman Cost) in $O(N \log N)$ time}]
#include <cstdint>
#include <functional>
#include <queue>
#include <span>
#include <vector>

[[nodiscard]] std::int64_t min_rope_merge_cost(std::span<const std::int64_t> ropes) {
    if (ropes.size() <= 1) return 0; // Zero merges needed for 0 or 1 rope

    std::priority_queue<std::int64_t, std::vector<std::int64_t>, std::greater<>> min_heap(
        std::greater<>{}, std::vector<std::int64_t>(ropes.begin(), ropes.end())
    );

    std::int64_t total_cost = 0;
    while (min_heap.size() > 1) {
        const std::int64_t first = min_heap.top();
        min_heap.pop();
        const std::int64_t second = min_heap.top();
        min_heap.pop();

        const std::int64_t merged = first + second;
        total_cost += merged;
        min_heap.push(merged);
    }
    return total_cost;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Two-Pointer Greedy Matching}

Sorting both arrays ascending lets two indices march linearly in $O(m + n)$ after the $O(m \log m + n \log n)$ sort. Whenever \texttt{supplies[j] >= demands[i]}, we lock in a match and advance both; otherwise we discard the weak supply and advance only \texttt{j}.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Two-Pointer Greedy Matching in $O(m \log m + n \log n)$ time}]
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstddef>
#include <vector>

[[nodiscard]] std::size_t max_matched_tasks(std::vector<int> demands,
                                            std::vector<int> supplies) {
    std::ranges::sort(demands);
    std::ranges::sort(supplies);

    std::size_t i = 0; // Demand pointer
    std::size_t j = 0; // Supply pointer

    while (i < demands.size() && j < supplies.size()) {
        if (supplies[j] >= demands[i]) {
            ++i; // Demand satisfied!
        }
        ++j; // Supply either matched or too small for all remaining demands
    }
    return i;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\subsection{Furthest-Reach Jump Game (Reachability and Minimum Jumps)}

Both functions run in $O(N)$ time and $O(1)$ space. Notice that \texttt{min\_jumps} returns \texttt{std::optional<int>} so unreachable targets return \texttt{std::nullopt} instead of \texttt{-1}, and checks \texttt{i > max\_reach} to catch stalls immediately.

\begin{lstlisting}[caption={Jump Game reachability and minimum jumps in $O(N)$ time and $O(1)$ space}]
#include <algorithm>
#include <cstddef>
#include <optional>
#include <span>

[[nodiscard]] bool can_reach_end(std::span<const int> jumps) {
    std::size_t max_reach = 0;
    for (std::size_t i = 0; i < jumps.size(); ++i) {
        if (i > max_reach) return false; // Stranded before reaching index i
        if (jumps[i] > 0) {
            max_reach = std::max(max_reach, i + static_cast<std::size_t>(jumps[i]));
        }
        if (max_reach + 1 >= jumps.size()) return true;
    }
    return true;
}

[[nodiscard]] std::optional<int> min_jumps(std::span<const int> jumps) {
    if (jumps.size() <= 1) return 0;

    int count = 0;
    std::size_t current_end = 0;
    std::size_t max_reach = 0;

    for (std::size_t i = 0; i + 1 < jumps.size(); ++i) {
        if (i > max_reach) return std::nullopt; // Cannot reach index i
        if (jumps[i] > 0) {
            max_reach = std::max(max_reach, i + static_cast<std::size_t>(jumps[i]));
        }
        if (i == current_end) {
            if (max_reach <= current_end) return std::nullopt; // Horizon stalled
            ++count;
            current_end = max_reach;
            if (current_end + 1 >= jumps.size()) return count;
        }
    }
    return std::nullopt;
}
\end{lstlisting}

\section{Problem Pattern Recognition}
% ============================================================================

When an unfamiliar problem lands in front of you, run this three-step triage before writing any code:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{What does the problem ask for?} Extreme value (maximum count, minimum cost, minimum rooms/jumps/boats)---not counting all ways or enumerating every configuration.
    \item \textbf{What is the key ordering or priority?} Can you pick one sorting key (finish time, start time, value/weight ratio, size) or a dynamic min-heap priority?
    \item \textbf{Can you break it in 30 seconds?} Try a 3-element counterexample (like $\{1, 3, 4\}$ coins or indivisible items). If you cannot break it and an Exchange Argument swap holds, Greedy wins; if a counterexample breaks it, switch to Dynamic Programming.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Glossary}
% ============================================================================

\subsection*{Core Concepts}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Greedy algorithm}: An algorithm that constructs a solution step by step, irreversibly picking the locally best option at every step.
    \item \textbf{Local optimum}: The single best immediate choice available at the current step.
    \item \textbf{Global optimum}: The best possible overall solution across the entire problem.
    \item \textbf{Greedy choice property}: The guarantee that some globally optimal solution starts with the locally optimal greedy choice.
    \item \textbf{Optimal substructure}: The property that removing a greedy choice leaves a smaller, independent instance of the same optimization problem.
    \item \textbf{Exchange argument}: A proof technique that transforms any optimal solution $O^*$ into the greedy solution $G$ via validity-preserving swaps that never worsen the objective.
    \item \textbf{Greedy stays ahead}: A proof technique showing by induction that after every step $k$, the greedy solution's progress metric is at least as good as any optimal solution's.
    \item \textbf{Canonical coin system}: A set of denominations (such as US coins $\{1, 5, 10, 25\}$) where largest-coin-first greedy always yields the minimum coin count.
    \item \textbf{Min-heap (priority queue)}: A binary-heap container supporting $O(1)$ inspection of the smallest element and $O(\log N)$ insertion/removal.
    \item \textbf{Horizon (furthest reach)}: The rightmost index $R_{\max}$ reachable from any position scanned so far in a linear jump or interval-cover pass.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Mathematical Symbols}
\begin{itemize}
    \item $N$: Number of elements, intervals, items, or ropes in the input.
    \item $G,\; O^*$: The greedy solution and an arbitrary globally optimal solution.
    \item $[s_i, f_i)$: Half-open interval starting at $s_i$ and ending at $f_i$ (touching endpoints do not overlap).
    \item $f_{\text{last}}$: Finish time of the most recently accepted interval.
    \item $K_{\min}$: Minimum number of partitions (rooms) needed to schedule all overlapping intervals.
    \item $W,\; v_i,\; w_i,\; \rho_i$: Knapsack weight capacity, item value, item weight, and value density $\rho_i = v_i / w_i$.
    \item $L_i,\; d_i$: Initial rope length (or symbol frequency) and its leaf depth in the merge/Huffman tree.
    \item $R_{\max},\; E_{\text{cur}}$: Furthest reachable index so far and boundary of the current jump layer.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Practice Sheet: Mixed Pen-and-Paper Drills}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Practice Sheet: Mixed Pen-and-Paper Drills}
\markboth{Practice Sheet: Mixed Pen-and-Paper Drills}{}
% ============================================================================

These six cold-start drills mix all six thinking modes with zero hints about which subsection applies. Work through them on scratch paper, then check the Solutions section.

\begin{tryitbox}{Triage}{Greedy or Dynamic Programming}{ex9}
For each disguised scenario below, state whether \textbf{Greedy} or \textbf{Dynamic Programming} applies, and name the exact sorting key or counterexample:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \emph{Scenario A}: Balloons span horizontal segments $[x_{\text{start}}, x_{\text{end}}]$. A vertical arrow shot at $x$ bursts every balloon with $x_{\text{start}} \le x \le x_{\text{end}}$. Find the \textbf{minimum arrows} to burst all balloons.
    \item \emph{Scenario B}: Postage stamps come in values $\{1, 5, 7\}$. Find the minimum stamps summing to $10$.
    \item \emph{Scenario C}: You have a $100\text{ L}$ fuel tank and five divisible fuel canisters of varying energy-per-liter. Maximize total energy loaded.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Trace}{Circular Gas Station Tour}{ex10}
Five stations sit on a circular track with \texttt{gas = [1, 2, 3, 4, 5]} and travel costs \texttt{cost = [3, 4, 5, 1, 2]} to reach the next station. Compute the net delta $g_i - c_i$ at each station, run the prefix-surplus reset greedy rule, and identify the unique valid starting index.
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Debug}{Two Subtle Heap and Sorting Bugs}{ex11}
Identify the exact flaw in each snippet:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \emph{Snippet 1 (Rope Merging)}: Sorts \texttt{ropes} ascending once, then loops \texttt{acc += ropes[i]; total += acc;} for \texttt{i = 1 .. n - 1}. Give the smallest array where this returns the wrong cost.
    \item \emph{Snippet 2 (Min Meeting Rooms)}: Sorts meetings by \textbf{end time} instead of start time before pushing into the min-heap. Give a 4-meeting input where this miscounts rooms.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Twist}{Weighted Interval Scheduling}{ex12}
Now suppose every interval also has a \textbf{profit} $p_i$: $A = [1, 3)$ with $p_A = 2$, $B = [2, 5)$ with $p_B = 10$, and $C = [4, 6)$ with $p_C = 2$. You want to maximize \emph{total profit} of non-overlapping intervals. What does earliest-finish greedy pick, what is the true optimum, and which paradigm is needed now?
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Edge Cases}{Stress-Test Three Greedy Functions}{ex13}
Predict the exact output of \texttt{max\_events}, \texttt{min\_meeting\_rooms}, and \texttt{min\_jumps} on these killer edge cases:
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item \texttt{max\_events} on negative touching intervals $\{[-10, -5],\; [-5, 0],\; [-7, -2]\}$.
    \item \texttt{min\_meeting\_rooms} on identical intervals $\{[2, 5],\; [2, 5],\; [2, 5]\}$ and on an empty list $\{\}$.
    \item \texttt{min\_jumps} on a single-element array $\{0\}$ and on $\{0, 2, 3\}$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{tryitbox}{Derive}{Shortest Processing Time First}{ex14}
A single server must execute $N$ jobs with durations $t_1, \dots, t_N$ back-to-back. If job $k$ finishes at completion time $C_k = \sum_{j=1}^{k} t_j$, we want to minimize total completion time $\sum_{k=1}^{N} C_k$.
\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
    \item Test all $3! = 6$ orderings (or the two extremes) for durations $[5, 2, 3]$ to spot the optimal order.
    \item Use a 2-element \textbf{Exchange Argument}: suppose an ordering has an adjacent inverted pair with $t_a > t_b$. Compute how much $\sum C_k$ changes when you swap $a$ and $b$.
\end{enumerate}
\end{tryitbox}

\section*{Solutions to Pen-and-Paper Exercises}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Solutions to Pen-and-Paper Exercises}
\markboth{Solutions to Pen-and-Paper Exercises}{}
% ============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}[itemsep=1pt, parsep=1pt]
    \item \label{sol:ex1}\textbf{Exercise 1 (Debug) --- Break Two Plausible Interval Rules:}
    \emph{Rule A (earliest start)} fails on $[0, 10)$, $[1, 3)$, $[4, 6)$: picks $[0, 10)$ ($1$ event) instead of $[1, 3)$, $[4, 6)$ ($2$ events).
    \emph{Rule B (shortest duration)} fails on $[1, 5)$, $[4, 6)$, $[5, 9)$: picks $[4, 6)$ ($1$ event) instead of $[1, 5)$, $[5, 9)$ ($2$ events).
    \trapnote{A greedy rule that works on 90\% of random tests is still wrong---always hunt for a 3-element bridge interval that blocks two neighbors.}

    \item \label{sol:ex2}\textbf{Exercise 2 (Trace) --- Earliest Finish vs. Minimum Rooms:}
    (1) Sorted by finish: $[2, 3)$, $[1, 5)$, $[3, 6)$, $[6, 7)$, $[5, 8)$. Picks $[2, 3) \to [3, 6) \to [6, 7)$: \textbf{3 events}.
    (2) Sorted by start: $[1, 5)$, $[2, 3)$, $[3, 6)$, $[5, 8)$, $[6, 7)$. Heap states: $\{5\} \to \{3, 5\} \to \{5, 6\} \to \{6, 8\} \to \{7, 8\}$. Final size: \textbf{2 rooms}.
    \trapnote{Confusing the two interval sorting keys: max non-overlapping sorts by \emph{finish} time; minimum rooms sorts by \emph{start} time.}

    \item \label{sol:ex3}\textbf{Exercise 3 (Edge Cases) --- Zero-Weight and NaN Trap:}
    (1) Take $(15, 0)$ for free ($+15$, $W_{\text{rem}} = 10$), ignore $(0, 0)$, take $(20, 5)$ ($\rho = 4$, $+20$, $W_{\text{rem}} = 5$), take half of $(30, 10)$ ($\rho = 3$, $+15$, $W_{\text{rem}} = 0$). Total $= \mathbf{50.0}$.
    (2) Item $(0, 0)$ computes $0.0 / 0.0 = \texttt{NaN}$; comparing \texttt{NaN} against any ratio is always \texttt{false}, breaking transitivity of equivalence in \texttt{std::ranges::sort}.
    \trapnote{Assuming IEEE~754 \texttt{+Inf} handles all zero weights---it handles \texttt{positive / 0.0}, but \texttt{0.0 / 0.0} silently yields \texttt{NaN} and Undefined Behavior.}

    \item \label{sol:ex4}\textbf{Exercise 4 (Trace) --- Min-Heap Rope Merging:}
    Start $\{2, 4, 7, 9, 10\}$. (1) Pop $2, 4 \to +6$, push $6$: $\{6, 7, 9, 10\}$. (2) Pop $6, 7 \to +13$, push $13$: $\{9, 10, 13\}$. (3) Pop $9, 10 \to +19$, push $19$: $\{13, 19\}$. (4) Pop $13, 19 \to +32$, push $32$: $\{32\}$. Total cost $= 6 + 13 + 19 + 32 = \mathbf{70}$.
    \trapnote{Forgetting to compare the newly pushed $13$ against $9$ and $10$---if you merged $13 + 9$ next instead of $9 + 10$, total cost jumps to $73$.}

    \item \label{sol:ex5}\textbf{Exercise 5 (Twist) --- Boats to Save People:}
    Use \textbf{opposite-direction two pointers} ($l = 0, r = n - 1$): always seat the heaviest person $w[r]$ and pair them with the lightest person $w[l]$ if $w[l] + w[r] \le 3$. On $[1, 2, 2, 3]$: $(3)$ alone ($r \to 2$), $(1, 2)$ together ($l \to 1, r \to 1$), $(2)$ alone $\implies \mathbf{3}$ boats.
    \trapnote{Pairing the two lightest people $(1, 2)$ first can leave heavier people stranded alone in larger inputs (e.g., limit $5$ with $[1, 2, 3, 4]$: pairing $(1, 4)$ and $(2, 3)$ takes $2$ boats, whereas pairing $(1, 2)$ first forces $3$ boats!).}

    \item \label{sol:ex6}\textbf{Exercise 6 (Trace) --- Minimum Jumps Horizon:}
    $i=0$: $R_{\max} = 2$, hit $E_{\text{cur}} = 0 \to \text{jumps} = 1, E_{\text{cur}} = 2$.
    $i=1$: $R_{\max} = \max(2, 1{+}3) = 4$.
    $i=2$: $R_{\max} = \max(4, 2{+}0) = 4$, hit $E_{\text{cur}} = 2 \to \text{jumps} = 2, E_{\text{cur}} = 4 \ge 4$ (done!). Answer: \textbf{2 jumps}.
    \trapnote{Panicking at $A[2] = 0$: you never had to land on index $2$; index $1$ inside jump~1's window already pushed $R_{\max}$ past the zero.}

    \item \label{sol:ex7}\textbf{Exercise 7 (Derive) --- Total Merge Cost as Weighted Leaf Depth:}
    Merges on $[1, 2, 3, 4]$: $1{+}2=3$ ($\{3,3,4\}$), $3{+}3=6$ ($\{4,6\}$), $4{+}6=10$. Sum of merges $= 3 + 6 + 10 = \mathbf{19}$.
    Tree leaf depths: $d(1) = 3$, $d(2) = 3$, $d(3) = 2$, $d(4) = 1$. Weighted sum $= 1(3) + 2(3) + 3(2) + 4(1) = 3 + 6 + 6 + 4 = \mathbf{19}$. Exact match!
    \trapnote{Counting edges vs. nodes when measuring depth: the root has depth $0$, its children have depth $1$, etc.---depth equals the exact number of merges an item survives.}

    \item \label{sol:ex8}\textbf{Exercise 8 (Debug) --- Spot Two Bugs in Broken Jump Code:}
    Bug 1 (loops through last index): on $\{2, 1, 1\}$ ($N = 3$), at $i=0$ sets $E_{\text{cur}}=2$, $\text{count}=1$; then at $i=2$ triggers $i == E_{\text{cur}}$ again and returns \textbf{2} instead of \textbf{1} (even on $\{0\}$ it returns $1$ instead of $0$!).
    Bug 2 (ignores stalled horizon): on $\{1, 0, 5\}$, at $i=1$ $R_{\max}$ stays $1$, then $i=2 > 1$ illegally reads $A[2] = 5$ as if reachable and returns \textbf{2} instead of unreachable (\texttt{std::nullopt}).
    \trapnote{Forgetting both loop bounds: stop at \texttt{i + 1 < n}, and always verify \texttt{i <= max\_reach} (and \texttt{max\_reach > current\_end}) before taking a jump.}

    \item \label{sol:ex9}\textbf{Exercise 9 (Triage) --- Greedy or Dynamic Programming:}
    \emph{Scenario A}: \textbf{Greedy} (sort balloons by $x_{\text{end}}$ ascending and shoot at the earliest $x_{\text{end}}$---identical to Interval Scheduling!).
    \emph{Scenario B}: \textbf{Dynamic Programming} (greedy picks $7 + 1 + 1 + 1 = 4$ stamps; optimal DP picks $5 + 5 = 2$ stamps).
    \emph{Scenario C}: \textbf{Greedy} (Fractional Knapsack: sort canisters descending by energy per liter).
    \trapnote{Misclassifying ``minimum arrows'' as minimum rooms: shooting one arrow through overlapping balloons is the dual of picking maximum disjoint intervals (sort by \emph{end} coordinate).}

    \item \label{sol:ex10}\textbf{Exercise 10 (Trace) --- Circular Gas Station Tour:}
    Deltas $g_i - c_i = [-2, -2, -2, +3, +3]$. Total sum $= 0 \ge 0$, so a solution exists.
    Start at $s = 0$: tank $= -2 < 0 \to$ reset $s = 1$, tank $= 0$. At $i = 1$: $-2 < 0 \to s = 2$. At $i = 2$: $-2 < 0 \to s = 3$. At $i = 3$: $+3 \ge 0$. At $i = 4$: $+6 \ge 0$. Unique valid start: \textbf{index 3}.
    \trapnote{Forgetting to check that total $\sum (g_i - c_i) \ge 0$: if the total net surplus is negative, no starting station works even if the final suffix is positive.}

    \item \label{sol:ex11}\textbf{Exercise 11 (Debug) --- Two Subtle Heap and Sorting Bugs:}
    (1) Fails on $[2, 2, 3, 3]$ (or $[3, 4, 5, 6]$): static linear merging gives $4 + 7 + 10 = 21$, whereas a min-heap merges $2{+}2=4$, $3{+}3=6$, $4{+}6=10 \implies \mathbf{20}$.
    (2) Fails on $[0, 1), [0, 2), [2, 4), [1, 5)$ (already sorted by end time): $[0, 1) \to \{1\}$, $[0, 2) \to \{1, 2\}$, then $[2, 4)$ steals room $1$ ($\{2, 4\}$), forcing $[1, 5)$ (which sees $2 > 1$) to open a \textbf{3rd room} $\{2, 4, 5\}$---even though true peak overlap is \textbf{2} (Room~1: $[0, 1) \to [1, 5)$; Room~2: $[0, 2) \to [2, 4)$).
    \trapnote{Both bugs stem from processing items out of dynamic priority order: rope sums change relative order after merging, and min-heap room reuse requires chronological start-time arrivals so a later meeting never steals an earlier room.}

    \item \label{sol:ex12}\textbf{Exercise 12 (Twist) --- Weighted Interval Scheduling:}
    Earliest-finish greedy picks $A = [1, 3)$ and $C = [4, 6)$ for profit $2 + 2 = \mathbf{4}$, missing $B = [2, 5)$ with profit $\mathbf{10}$. Because profits break the unweighted swap test, you must use \textbf{Dynamic Programming} (sort by finish time + binary search for the latest non-overlapping predecessor: $O(N \log N)$).
    \trapnote{Assuming a greedy rule survives adding arbitrary weights---weighted intervals require DP (or a max-weight path on a DAG).}

    \item \label{sol:ex13}\textbf{Exercise 13 (Edge Cases) --- Stress-Test Three Greedy Functions:}
    (1) \texttt{max\_events} sorts $[-10, -5], [-7, -2], [-5, 0]$ and accepts $[-10, -5]$ and $[-5, 0] \implies \mathbf{2}$ (fails if \texttt{current\_end} starts at \texttt{-1}!).
    (2) \texttt{min\_meeting\_rooms} returns \textbf{3} on three identical intervals and \textbf{0} on $\{\}$.
    (3) \texttt{min\_jumps} returns \textbf{0} on $\{0\}$ (already at index $N-1$) and \textbf{\texttt{std::nullopt}} (unreachable) on $\{0, 2, 3\}$.
    \trapnote{Forgetting the trivial size-1 base case in Jump Game: an array of length $1$ with value $0$ is already at the finish line ($0$ jumps).}

    \item \label{sol:ex14}\textbf{Exercise 14 (Derive) --- Shortest Processing Time First:}
    (1) On $[5, 2, 3]$: sorted ascending $[2, 3, 5]$ gives completion times $2 + 5 + 10 = \mathbf{17}$ (minimum); descending $[5, 3, 2]$ gives $5 + 8 + 10 = 23$.
    (2) Swapping an adjacent inverted pair $(t_a, t_b)$ with $t_a > t_b$ leaves all jobs before and after unchanged, while changing the pair's combined completion contribution by $(t_b - t_a) < 0$ (strictly smaller!). Repeating swaps transforms any schedule into ascending order.
    \trapnote{Adding job durations instead of cumulative completion times---each job's duration is paid once for itself and once for every job waiting behind it.}
\end{enumerate}
